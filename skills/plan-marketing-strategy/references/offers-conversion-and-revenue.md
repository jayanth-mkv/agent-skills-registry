# Offers, Conversion, and Revenue

Use this reference to design the value exchange, improve conversion paths, plan pricing or packaging, connect marketing to sales, and grow revenue without separating acquisition from customer value.

## 1. Offer architecture

An offer is more than a product and price. Define:

- priority audience and triggering situation;
- progress or outcome promised;
- product/service and scope;
- mechanism or experience;
- proof and qualification;
- price or other value exchange;
- effort, delay, switching cost, and perceived risk;
- guarantee, trial, demonstration, consultation, or support where appropriate;
- urgency grounded in real timing or capacity;
- next step and what happens afterward;
- exclusions and who should not buy.

Test offer strength as:

```text
valuable outcome × relevance × credibility
-------------------------------------------
money + effort + time + uncertainty + risk
```

Use the relationship only as a reasoning aid; do not invent numerical scores.

## 2. Entry offers and lead capture

Choose an entry step proportionate to intent and buying motion:

- ungated utility or education;
- newsletter or recurring insight;
- template, assessment, calculator, or tool;
- sample, trial, demo, consultation, or event;
- waitlist, preorder, application, or quote;
- direct purchase.

Do not add a lead magnet merely to collect addresses. It should produce real value, identify a meaningful need, and lead naturally to the next customer decision.

For every capture mechanism specify:

- promise and delivery;
- minimum necessary data;
- consent and expected follow-up;
- qualification logic;
- immediate experience;
- nurture, sales, or product handoff;
- suppression and deletion;
- measurement through downstream quality.

Avoid forced gates, bait-and-switch, auto-enrollment, prechecked consent, and fabricated assessment results.

## 3. Pricing and packaging

Separate:

- **metric:** what usage or value dimension price follows;
- **structure:** subscription, usage, transaction, seat/access entitlement, service, bundle, or hybrid;
- **packages:** which capabilities, service, limits, and buyer each tier serves;
- **price level:** amount and billing period;
- **presentation:** anchoring, comparison, defaults, and explanation;
- **policy:** trials, discounts, renewals, refunds, overages, taxes, and changes.

Gather evidence from willingness-to-pay research, sales and loss reasons, product usage, cost-to-serve, expansion behavior, competitor alternatives, and controlled tests. Competitor price is context, not the answer.

Check:

- value metric aligns price with customer success;
- tiers reflect distinct needs rather than arbitrary feature removal;
- limits are understandable and operationally enforceable;
- margins and service capacity support the promise;
- upgrade and downgrade paths are fair;
- localized pricing, tax, currency, and accessibility are handled;
- existing customers and renewals receive clear treatment.

Do not recommend a precise price from generic benchmarks alone.

For an enterprise or negotiated pricing change, write a commercial protocol:

- affected products, segments, territories, currencies, and contract types;
- list price, floors, discount authority, approval, and quote-validity window;
- treatment of active evaluations, signed quotes, renewals, existing customers, and exceptions;
- package, entitlement, service, overage, implementation, and support changes;
- contract, billing, finance, tax, CRM, sales-compensation, and reporting dependencies;
- customer notice, negotiation, migration, grandfathering, downgrade, and support paths;
- fairness and consistency checks across comparable accounts;
- pilot cohort, comparison, outcome maturity, guardrails, and rollback;
- accountable commercial, finance, operations, customer, and qualified-review owners.

Avoid casual account-level price experiments inside active negotiations. Use research, controlled offer or presentation tests where appropriate, bounded new-customer pilots, or staged market/segment rollout with explicit comparability and authority.

## 4. Conversion diagnosis

Define the page or flow's job and eligible audience. Inspect:

- audience-source and intent mix;
- promise continuity from source to destination;
- first-screen comprehension;
- offer clarity and proof;
- objection and risk handling;
- information scent and decision hierarchy;
- form, checkout, signup, demo, or contact effort;
- error states, speed, mobile behavior, accessibility, and localization;
- price, shipping, availability, trust, and policy surprises;
- post-conversion expectation and fulfillment;
- downstream activation, quality, retention, and support.

Use behavioral evidence, recordings where consent permits, usability tests, support/sales notes, form errors, path analysis, and segmented conversion. Do not infer causality from a generic checklist.

## 5. Journey design

Map:

```text
entry context -> promise -> evidence -> offer -> action
-> confirmation -> first value -> follow-up -> retained value
```

At each transition define:

- customer question;
- information or reassurance required;
- primary action and alternative path;
- owner and service level;
- event and failure state;
- recovery mechanism.

Remove steps that serve internal convenience without creating necessary qualification, safety, or customer value. Preserve steps that prevent a harmful mismatch even if they lower headline conversion.

For paywalls or upgrade prompts, connect the ask to experienced value and a relevant limit. Do not manufacture obstruction solely to force payment.

## 6. Sales and revenue handoffs

For sales-assisted journeys define:

- marketing-qualified behavior, not only demographic score;
- disqualification and suppression;
- routing, territory, ownership, and SLA;
- context passed to sales;
- accepted, rejected, recycled, and duplicate handling;
- sales stages and exit criteria;
- nurture or product branch;
- closed-won onboarding;
- loss and objection feedback to marketing.

Create enablement for real conversations:

- discovery guide;
- value and proof narrative;
- role-specific one-pager;
- demo plan;
- objection evidence;
- comparison guidance;
- business-case model with transparent assumptions;
- follow-up templates;
- handoff checklist.

Do not fabricate personalization, urgency, customer evidence, or competitor claims.

## 7. Expansion and monetization

Start from delivered value. Identify:

- usage depth and successful outcomes;
- adjacent jobs or teams;
- natural capacity or value thresholds;
- renewal risk and service health;
- cross-sell relevance;
- buyer and budget timing;
- customer effort and change management.

Expansion motions include usage growth, additional seats/locations, higher service level, add-ons, bundles, adjacent products, renewal terms, and advocacy. Select only those that improve customer fit and sustainable economics.

Measure incremental contribution, retained revenue, product adoption, downgrade/cancel behavior, support burden, and customer outcome. Do not use discounts to conceal weak value or create renewal shock.

## 8. Testing and safeguards

Prioritize tests by constraint:

- message or proof;
- offer structure;
- entry step;
- price presentation;
- form or checkout friction;
- trial or demo experience;
- sales routing;
- activation support;
- renewal or expansion timing.

Use revenue and customer-quality outcomes, not click-through alone. Monitor refunds, complaints, support, accessibility, fraud, margin, retention, and segment harm.

For pricing or policy changes, plan migration, notification, customer support, billing correctness, legal review, rollback, and cohort analysis. Never implement a high-impact price or billing change without explicit authorization.
