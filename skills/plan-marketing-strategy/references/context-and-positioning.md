# Context, Research, and Positioning

Use this reference to learn enough about the market and customer to make defensible choices. Do not make completion of a large questionnaire a prerequisite for useful work.

## 1. Adaptive intake

Start with the smallest set that changes the strategy:

- product, service, or behavior being marketed;
- business model, price or value exchange, and sales motion;
- desired outcome, baseline, horizon, and why it matters now;
- current audience and strongest evidence about them;
- current journey, channels, results, and known failure point;
- differentiators, proof, objections, and prohibited claims;
- budget, team, skills, tooling, timing, and operational constraints;
- geography, language, accessibility, privacy, consent, and regulated-category constraints.

For a new venture, prioritize problem evidence, segment access, willingness to act, alternatives, and founder distribution advantages. For an operating business, prioritize cohort behavior, win/loss evidence, conversion paths, retention, unit economics, and channel history.

Ask one compact batch of material questions when needed. Explain which choice each answer affects. When answers are unavailable, proceed with scenarios rather than pretending certainty.

## 2. Evidence hierarchy

Weight evidence roughly in this order:

1. Observed customer behavior, purchases, retention, product usage, and reliable experiments.
2. Direct research: interviews, support conversations, sales calls, win/loss notes, surveys with known sampling.
3. First-party analytics, CRM, search-console, campaign, and financial data with defined events and windows.
4. Market data, primary documents, public customer behavior, and verified competitor artifacts.
5. Practitioner judgment and comparable cases.
6. Team opinions, generic benchmarks, and agent-generated hypotheses.

Evidence quality also depends on recency, representativeness, sample size, measurement integrity, and relevance to the decision. A large irrelevant dataset can be weaker than a small set of directly relevant interviews.

Maintain three lists:

- **Known:** supported well enough to use.
- **Assumed:** plausible and temporarily usable.
- **Unknown:** material gap requiring research or a test.

## 3. Research plan

Research only until the next decision becomes clear. Use the lowest-cost method that can reduce important uncertainty:

| Uncertainty | Useful evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Does the problem matter? | Interviews, observed workarounds, search behavior, support/sales evidence |
| Which segment feels it most? | Cohort outcomes, win/loss, firmographic or behavioral segmentation |
| What language resonates? | Verbatim calls, reviews, communities, queries, support logs |
| Why choose or reject us? | Decision interviews, sales objections, churn/cancel reasons |
| Where can we reach them? | Referral sources, communities, search demand, media habits, partner overlap |
| Will the offer convert? | Concierge test, landing test, sales conversations, preorders where appropriate |
| Can the channel scale? | Unit economics, inventory, content supply, saturation, operational capacity |

When browsing, favor primary sources and recent evidence. Record URLs and dates. Never invent customer quotes, market size, competitor features, pricing, rankings, or demand.

## 4. Audience model

Select a priority segment based on more than demographics. Capture:

- **Situation:** context and trigger that makes progress urgent.
- **Job:** functional, emotional, and social progress sought.
- **Pain and stakes:** current cost, risk, delay, or frustration.
- **Alternatives:** competitors, internal build, manual work, delay, or doing nothing.
- **Decision unit:** user, champion, buyer, approver, blocker, and influencer.
- **Awareness:** problem, solution, product, and offer knowledge.
- **Access:** places, queries, relationships, platforms, and moments that expose the segment.
- **Value and fit:** outcome value, frequency, retention potential, serviceability, and margin.
- **Disqualifiers:** who should not be targeted and why.

For multi-sided products, model each side separately and show the dependency. For enterprise buying, distinguish individual pain from organizational purchase criteria. For consumer products, distinguish initial motivation from repeat-use motivation.

## 5. Positioning

Derive positioning from evidence:

```text
For [priority audience in a triggering situation],
[product] is the [frame of reference]
that enables [valuable outcome].
Unlike [real alternative], it [meaningful difference],
supported by [reasons to believe].
```

Treat this as a reasoning scaffold, not mandatory public copy. Test:

- Is the category understandable without a lecture?
- Is the audience narrow enough to recognize itself?
- Is the outcome important and concrete?
- Is the alternative real?
- Is the difference relevant, defensible, and provable?
- Does the product experience deliver the promise?

If the market is mature, clarity and credible differentiation usually matter more than novelty. If the category is new, budget extra work for education and risk reduction.

## 6. Messaging and proof

Build a message hierarchy:

1. Audience and situation.
2. Problem or desired progress.
3. Promise and outcome.
4. Mechanism: why this solution can deliver.
5. Differentiation from the current alternative.
6. Proof: demonstration, data, customer evidence, credentials, process, or guarantee.
7. Objection response.
8. Next step and expectation.

Create a message matrix when multiple decision roles or awareness stages exist:

| Segment or role | Trigger | Desired outcome | Main objection | Message | Proof | CTA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use verified specificity. Qualify results and testimonials. Do not convert one customer's result into a universal claim. Do not create false consensus, false urgency, or unverified superlatives.

## 7. Competitive analysis

Analyze alternatives to make choices, not to produce a feature dump:

- audience and use case each alternative prioritizes;
- category and promise;
- acquisition and sales motion;
- offer, pricing, packaging, and switching cost;
- proof, trust signals, and objections addressed;
- customer praise and complaints from attributable sources;
- product or operational advantage;
- gaps that matter to the priority audience.

Separate direct competitors, substitutes, internal workarounds, and no-decision. Do not infer revenue, performance, traffic, or customer sentiment without evidence. A competitor's visible tactic does not prove it works.

## 8. Context brief

Summarize only the context needed for execution:

```markdown
## Decision
## Business and offer
## Priority audience and trigger
## Job, pain, alternatives, and objections
## Position, promise, differentiation, and proof
## Journey and current constraint
## Brand voice and prohibited claims
## Goals, baselines, constraints, and non-goals
## Known / assumed / unknown
## Sources and recency
```

Reuse an existing reliable brief when available. Update stale facts. Do not create a new permanent file unless the user asks or repeated work makes it valuable.
