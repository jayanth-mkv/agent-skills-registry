## Start with audience demand

Define:

- priority audience and their level of knowledge;
- problem, trigger, job, constraint, and decision;
- journey stage and desired next action;
- language, geography, device, and seasonality;
- business relevance and disqualifiers;
- what evidence the organization can uniquely provide.

Use customer interviews, sales/support language, site search, GSC queries, community discussions, product data, and market research. Keyword tools estimate demand; they do not define the audience by themselves.

Frame an opportunity:

```text
For [audience] trying to [job] under [constraint],
create or improve [page/experience] that provides [unique value],
supports [business outcome], and will be evaluated by [signals].
```

## Research the search landscape

### Build a query universe

Collect:

- branded and non-branded queries;
- problem, task, comparison, alternative, category, product, and support queries;
- local and “near me” variants when location matters;
- questions and follow-ups;
- image/video/news/shopping queries;
- terminology used by beginners and experts;
- existing GSC queries, including unexpected ones;
- zero-search-volume first-party needs when strategically important.

Cluster by shared intent and required answer, not just lexical similarity. One page may serve several variants; several intents may require separate experiences.

### Sample SERPs responsibly

For each priority cluster, record:

- query, locale, language, device, and date;
- dominant and minority intents;
- result formats and search features;
- commercial/editorial/local/video/product mix;
- freshness sensitivity;
- top-result page types and source diversity;
- questions the result set answers or leaves unresolved;
- whether the user’s site has a credible right to win.

Results vary by context. Do not claim a universal “top ten” from one personalized sample. Use approved data providers or a controlled search environment when scale matters.

### Analyze competitors without copying

Compare:

- audience and job served;
- information architecture and time-to-value;
- claims, evidence, methodology, examples, tools, media, and usability;
- entities/subtopics and important relationships;
- demonstrated experience and author/reviewer transparency;
- freshness and maintenance;
- internal/external references;
- conversion path.

Classify gaps:

- **Expected:** necessary to satisfy the intent.
- **Differentiating:** evidence or utility competitors offer and the page lacks.
- **Commodity:** repeated material that needs only concise coverage.
- **Opportunity:** useful value the result set fails to provide.
- **Irrelevant:** competitor material that does not serve this audience.

Entity and semantic analysis is a planning heuristic, not proof of a hidden ranking formula.

## Map demand to pages

Build a map:

| Cluster/job | Intent | Existing URL | Page type | Business value | Performance | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Possible actions:

- keep and monitor;
- improve intent fit or evidence;
- expand a hub/spoke relationship;
- merge overlapping pages;
- split genuinely different intents;
- create a missing experience;
- redirect a replaced URL;
- retain but `noindex` for non-search utility;
- remove with an appropriate status when it has no user or search value.

Do not create one page per keyword variant. Do not merge pages merely because they share a query. Confirm audience, intent, content, links, conversions, and canonical behavior.

### Architecture

Use hubs and contextual paths that reflect user tasks and product taxonomy. Specify:

- hub purpose and included spokes;
- unique role of every page;
- parent/child and related relationships;
- link source, destination, placement, and suggested anchor;
- navigation/breadcrumb changes;
- pages that must not compete for the same job.

## Audit a page

### Establish page identity

State:

- page type and primary job;
- intended audience and search intent;
- target query cluster, if known;
- business/conversion role;
- current visibility and landing-page value;
- how success should be measured.

### Inspect the page

Evaluate:

1. **Eligibility and rendering** — status, directive, canonical, raw/rendered content.
2. **Intent fit** — does format and scope answer the dominant user need?
3. **Time-to-value** — how quickly can a user find the promised answer or action?
4. **Accuracy** — claims, dates, prices, specifications, citations, and limitations.
5. **Original value** — first-party data, experience, tools, examples, decisions, or analysis.
6. **Completeness** — essential questions and edge cases, without encyclopedia padding.
7. **Trust** — ownership, sourcing, methodology, author/reviewer context, contact/policy clarity where relevant.
8. **Structure** — descriptive title/H1/headings, readable flow, tables/lists/media when useful.
9. **Links** — helpful internal paths and trustworthy external corroboration.
10. **Presentation** — snippet candidates, images/video, truthful structured data, social preview.
11. **Action** — relevant CTA and a coherent next experience.
12. **Accessibility/mobile** — readable and operable for the intended audience.

Give exact locations and proposed repairs. Do not score content quality from word count or keyword frequency.

### Titles, headings, and snippets

- Write a unique, concise, accurate title that distinguishes the page and supports the click decision.
- Use an H1 that clearly identifies the visible page. Multiple H1s are not automatically a Google Search defect; prefer a coherent document structure for users and accessibility.
- Make headings descriptive and useful when scanned.
- Write a unique meta description when it helps summarize the page, but expect search systems to generate snippets from page content.
- Treat character counts as truncation heuristics, not ranking thresholds.
- Keep important answers in visible textual content; do not write solely for a snippet template.

## Create differentiated content

### Extract real expertise

Before writing high-value content, ask the subject-matter expert a few focused questions:

- What do people commonly get wrong?
- What happened in a real project, with verifiable specifics?
- What surprised you or failed?
- When does the advice not apply?
- What decision rule do you actually use?
- What evidence can be published, and what must remain confidential?

Never invent the answers. Preserve caveats and obtain approval for sensitive claims.

### Increase information value

Prefer:

- original measurements, research, and transparent methodology;
- first-hand tests, screenshots, photographs, or demonstrations;
- worked examples and decision tools;
- primary-source synthesis that adds analysis;
- limitations, failure cases, and alternatives;
- current product/pricing/legal facts verified at publication;
- downloadable or interactive utility when it solves the job.

Avoid summarizing the current SERP in new words. A page should have a reason to exist even if search traffic disappeared.

### Write for people and retrieval

- Answer the page’s main promise early.
- Use clear language, explicit nouns, and self-contained explanations where context could otherwise be lost.
- Define specialized terms and distinguish similar concepts.
- Support factual claims near the claim with trustworthy sources.
- Use tables, steps, examples, and media only when they improve comprehension.
- Make update dates reflect substantive updates.
- Keep consistent organization, product, author, and entity facts across owned profiles.
- Provide a useful next action without obstructing the content.

Do not mechanically force “citation blocks,” questions in every heading, or a universal passage length. Evaluate extractability as usability, not as a guaranteed AI-citation recipe.


Do not prescribe an exact length unless the format or user constraint requires it. Define the depth needed to resolve the user’s task.

## Refresh, consolidate, or retire

Build a content inventory with URL, template, purpose, topic/intent, owner, last substantive review, organic performance, conversions, links, index status, and quality risks.

Choose:

- **Keep:** still accurate, useful, distinct, and performing its role.
- **Refresh:** purpose remains valid; facts, examples, format, or coverage need improvement.
- **Reposition:** current intent or audience conflicts with demand/business fit.
- **Consolidate:** pages serve materially the same job and one stronger destination can preserve value.
- **Split:** one page poorly serves multiple distinct intents.
- **Retire:** no durable user/business value and no justified replacement.

Before consolidation or removal:

- inventory backlinks, internal links, traffic, conversions, and alternate uses;
- select a genuinely equivalent destination;
- define redirect/status behavior and update links/sitemaps;
- preserve needed content or records;
- create a rollback and monitoring plan.

Do not prune merely to make the site “fresh” or because a URL has low traffic. Low-demand support or trust pages can still be valuable.

## Internal linking

Use internal links to help people discover relevant next steps and to express site relationships.

For each recommendation name:

- source URL/template;
- destination URL;
- placement/context;
- proposed descriptive anchor;
- user reason for the link;
- expected coverage and owner.

Prioritize:

- links from relevant, authoritative, frequently visited/crawled pages;
- contextual paths between hubs and supporting pages;
- newly published pages that lack discovery;
- high-impression pages near meaningful opportunities;
- broken, redirected, or noncanonical link targets.

Avoid arbitrary “links per 1,000 words,” sitewide exact-match anchors, or automated insertion without editorial QA.

## Earn authority

Focus on legitimate relevance and reputation:

- publish primary research, useful tools, datasets, templates, or expert resources;
- contribute accurate expertise to relevant publications and communities;
- reclaim genuine unlinked mentions and repair broken references;
- maintain organization/person/product facts on authoritative owned and industry profiles;
- build partnerships, citations, reviews, and local references through real relationships;
- distribute content where the intended audience already participates;
- monitor brand accuracy and respond transparently.

Reject paid-link schemes, private networks, automated spam, fake profiles/reviews, irrelevant guest-post volume, hacked placements, and manipulative exchanges. Do not recommend disavowal from a vendor “toxicity” score alone; require evidence of a manual action or a documented manipulative-link history and follow current official guidance.

## Quality and myth controls

Reject or qualify these claims:

- “Use a 2% keyword density.” Write naturally; keyword stuffing is harmful.
- “Google prefers N words.” Match the user task; there is no preferred word count.
- “Every page needs exactly one H1 for Google.” Use clear structure; do not invent a ranking emergency.
- “Meta descriptions directly improve rankings.” They can influence presentation/click decisions but may be rewritten.
- “Duplicate content causes a penalty.” Duplication can waste resources or dilute signals; copying/spam is a separate concern.
- “E-E-A-T is a single ranking factor or score.” Use trust and expertise questions as quality diagnostics.
- “Schema makes a page rank.” It can enable understanding/features; it must be truthful and supported.
- “More pages create topical authority.” Low-value scaled pages can create risk and maintenance debt.
- “Two pages appearing for one query proves cannibalization.” Confirm split intent, performance instability, and a better portfolio design.
- “AI detectors prove authorship or quality.” Evaluate evidence, accuracy, originality, and usefulness directly.

Fact-check every specific statistic, quote, product claim, and current rule. If verification fails, remove it or mark it as unverified.
